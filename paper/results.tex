Figure~\ref{fig:passes} shows what a single pass keeps. On DecisionBench one-pass \pk{} gains $\gainSmallPkOneDb$ $\ciSmallPkOneDb$ at 0.8B and $\gainBigPkOneDb$ $\ciBigPkOneDb$ at 4B, against $\gainSmallPkDb$ and $\gainBigPkDb$ with eight passes; the one-pass labelled adapter gains $\gainSmallSupOneDb$ and $\gainBigSupOneDb$. At matched epochs the gap between verdicts and labels is $\gainSmallPkOneDbVsSupOne$ $\ciSmallPkOneDbVsSupOne$ and $\gainBigPkOneDbVsSupOne$ $\ciBigPkOneDbVsSupOne$, wider than with eight passes. On typed-decisions one pass gives $\gainSmallPkOneTyped$ and $\gainBigPkOneTyped$ (labels: $\gainSmallSupOneTyped$, $\gainBigSupOneTyped$; verdicts significantly behind at both sizes); on the public menu $\gainSmallPkOnePub$ and $\gainBigPkOnePub$; on emotion $\gainSmallPkOneEmo$ and $\gainBigPkOneEmo$; on AG News $\gainSmallPkOneAg$ and $\gainBigPkOneAg$. One pass keeps about half to two thirds of the eight-pass gain on DecisionBench and the public menu and more on emotion.

One pass confounds two things: each row is rated once, and the model takes one eighth of the gradient steps. The logged arms allow additional optimisation without new training verdicts, but also change the collection policy and use an off-policy objective. Each training row is answered once by the released model, rated once, and the add-on is then trained on that fixed log for eight passes, off-policy. With the released model's sampled answers and clipped importance weights this gains $\gainSmallLogIpsDb$ $\ciSmallLogIpsDb$ on DecisionBench at 0.8B and $\gainBigLogIpsDb$ $\ciBigLogIpsDb$ at 4B. Against one on-policy pass that is $\gainSmallLogIpsDbVsPkOne$ $\ciSmallLogIpsDbVsPkOne$ and $\gainBigLogIpsDbVsPkOne$ $\ciBigLogIpsDbVsPkOne$; against eight passes it is $\gainSmallLogIpsDbVsPk$ $\ciSmallLogIpsDbVsPk$ and $\gainBigLogIpsDbVsPk$ $\ciBigLogIpsDbVsPk$. On DecisionBench the logged arms have three seeds. Their point estimates recover part of the one-pass shortfall while remaining below eight fresh-feedback passes; the latter difference survives Holm adjustment at 0.8B. This is consistent with a benefit from additional verdicts, but is not a controlled isolation of information from policy and objective changes. On typed-decisions and emotion, where the logged arms have one seed, the logged-versus-eight-pass differences are not significant ($\gainSmallLogIpsTypedVsPk$ and $\gainSmallLogIpsEmoVsPk$ at 0.8B, $\gainBigLogIpsTypedVsPk$ and $\gainBigLogIpsEmoVsPk$ at 4B). Against the early-stopped labelled adapter the logged sampled arm is $\gainSmallLogIpsDbVsSupEs$ $\ciSmallLogIpsDbVsSupEs$ on DecisionBench, $\gainSmallLogIpsTypedVsSupEs$ on typed-decisions and $\gainSmallLogIpsEmoVsSupEs$ on emotion at 0.8B, and $\gainBigLogIpsDbVsSupEs$, $\gainBigLogIpsTypedVsSupEs$ and $\gainBigLogIpsEmoVsSupEs$ at 4B: one logged verdict per row stays below the labels on every cell, significantly on four of the six.

Table~\ref{tab:realistic} and Figure~\ref{fig:realistic} collect the deployment question. A system that serves its best answer rather than a sample logs only that answer's verdict: a 1 is a label, a 0 rules one option out. Learning from that log gains $\gainSmallLogArgDb$ $\ciSmallLogArgDb$ on DecisionBench at 0.8B and $\gainBigLogArgDb$ $\ciBigLogArgDb$ at 4B, $\gainSmallLogArgTyped$ and $\gainBigLogArgTyped$ on typed-decisions, and $\gainSmallLogArgEmo$ and $\gainBigLogArgEmo$ on emotion. It trails the early-stopped labels by $\absSmallLogArgDbVsSupEs$ and $\absBigLogArgDbVsSupEs$ points on DecisionBench and by $\absSmallLogArgEmoVsSupEs$ and $\absBigLogArgEmoVsSupEs$ on emotion, and on typed-decisions at 4B the difference from them is not significant ($\gainBigLogArgTypedVsSupEs$). Neither logging policy consistently dominates; the point-estimate advantages of sampled over best-answer logging are $\gainSmallLogIpsDbVsLogArg$ and $\gainBigLogIpsDbVsLogArg$ points more on DecisionBench and $\gainSmallLogIpsEmoVsLogArg$ on emotion at 0.8B, and $\gainBigLogIpsTypedVsLogArg$ on typed-decisions at 4B.

We measured damaged feedback only under the eight-pass protocol, where a flipped or missing verdict on one pass is redrawn on the next; how noise affects a single rating per row is not measured here. On DecisionBench, with three seeds, 20\% of verdicts flipped costs $\absSmallNoiseDbVsPk$ points at 0.8B and $\absBigNoiseDbVsPk$ at 4B, half of the answers unrated $\absSmallHalfDbVsPk$ and $\absBigHalfDbVsPk$, and complaint-skewed verdicts $\absSmallComplDbVsPk$ and $\absBigComplDbVsPk$. On typed-decisions and emotion these arms have one seed against the clean arm's three and differ from it by up to three points in either direction. One run failed: with half of the answers unrated on emotion at 4B the adapter's predictions are identical to the released model's on every test row and its reward stays flat through all eight passes, so that cell ($\gainBigHalfEmo$) says nothing about missing feedback. Rewards are computed from the gold answer in all of these; a rater who is systematically wrong about what was right is not simulated beyond the flipped verdicts.

\paragraph{Against VegaML's TTT head.}

After Holm adjustment the verdict-trained adapter is ahead of VegaML's labelled recipe on DecisionBench at both sizes and on AG News at 4B, without a significant difference on the public menu and typed-decisions, and behind on emotion at both sizes. On DecisionBench the guarded head answers $\tttCoveredSmallDb$ of 1,075 rows at 0.8B and $\tttCoveredBigDb$ at 4B (Table~\ref{tab:ttt}). On the rows it answers, \pk{} minus TTT is $\gainPeekabooVsTttCoveredSmallDb$ $\ciPeekabooVsTttCoveredSmallDb$ and $\gainPeekabooVsTttCoveredBigDb$ $\ciPeekabooVsTttCoveredBigDb$, neither significant, and the labelled adapter does about as well there ($\gainAdapterVsTttCoveredSmallDb$, $\gainAdapterVsTttCoveredBigDb$). Emotion goes to TTT by a wide margin: a six-way single-label task with 3,200 labelled rows is where a logistic head on good features is at its best.

\paragraph{How much the protocol reveals.}
Table~\ref{tab:anatomy} (right) counts what eight on-policy passes tell the learner. On DecisionBench at 0.8B each training row produces $\revealsSmallPkDb$ reward-1 events on average, each of which names the gold answer; from the released model's own probabilities, 8 draws would hit the gold at least once on a share $\prevealSmallPkDb$ of the rows, and $\binarySmallPkDb$ of the rows have two options, where any verdict is the label. On typed-decisions the figures are $\revealsSmallPkTyped$ reveals per row and $\prevealSmallPkTyped$; on emotion $\revealsSmallPkEmo$ and $\prevealSmallPkEmo$. The first pass's reward, measured before each training row is revisited, is $\passoneSmallPkDb$ on DecisionBench against the released model's best-answer accuracy of $\baseargmaxSmallPkDb$ on the same rows: this is a descriptive sampled-versus-best-answer comparison on the feedback pool. It does not bound cumulative exploration cost or subsequent deployment regressions, and earlier batches may already have updated the policy before a later row is first sampled.

\paragraph{A label prior with no input.}
Per-task log-offsets on the released probabilities, one per (task, option text), trained with \pk{}'s own loop from verdicts, gain $\gainPriorFbSmallTyped$ on typed-decisions at 0.8B and $\gainPriorFbBigTyped$ at 4B, against \pk{}'s $\gainSmallPkTyped$ and $\gainBigPkTyped$; \pk{} minus the prior is $\gainPkVsPriorFbSmallTyped$ $\ciPkVsPriorFbSmallTyped$ and $\gainPkVsPriorFbBigTyped$ $\ciPkVsPriorFbBigTyped$. About two thirds of the typed-decisions gain at 0.8B, and all of it at 4B, is a prior shift that 71 numbers can learn from verdicts. On the public menu the prior gains $\gainPriorFbSmallPub$ and $\gainPriorFbBigPub$ (\pk{} minus prior $\gainPkVsPriorFbSmallPub$, $\gainPkVsPriorFbBigPub$). On DecisionBench the prior gains $\gainPriorFbSmallDb$ and $\gainPriorFbBigDb$ against \pk{}'s $\gainSmallPkDb$ and $\gainBigPkDb$, so there the adapter learns something the option text alone does not give (\pk{} minus prior $\gainPkVsPriorFbSmallDb$ $\ciPkVsPriorFbSmallDb$, $\gainPkVsPriorFbBigDb$ $\ciPkVsPriorFbBigDb$); on emotion the prior gains $\gainPriorFbSmallEmo$ and $\gainPriorFbBigEmo$ against $\gainSmallPkEmo$ and $\gainBigPkEmo$. DecisionBench and emotion are the benchmarks on which the paper's claim about learning from verdicts rests; typed-decisions and the public menu are largely prior correction, which verdicts also deliver, but which the per-task offset model can also deliver (71 parameters on typed-decisions and 536 on the public menu).

\paragraph{Which rows change.}
Table~\ref{tab:fixbreak} and Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy} (left) test the prediction of the $\pi(y)$ weighting. Over the benchmarks with a training split, eight-pass \pk{} fixes $\FixMeanSmallPk$\% of the released model's wrong rows at 0.8B and breaks $\BreakMeanSmallPk$\% of its right ones; the labelled adapter fixes $\FixMeanSmallSup$\% and breaks $\BreakMeanSmallSup$\%. Where the released model ranked the gold option second, \pk{} fixes $\FixRankTwoMeanSmallPk$\% of rows against the adapter's $\FixRankTwoMeanSmallSup$\%; where it ranked it fourth or lower, $\FixRankFourMeanSmallPk$\% against $\FixRankFourMeanSmallSup$\%. The verdict-trained adapter is the more conservative learner: it fixes fewer of the deep errors, consistent with the $\pi(y)$-weighted expected gradient, and breaks fewer correct answers. The two differences partly offset each other, which is why the net accuracies are close; at DecisionBench's base accuracy the fixing deficit is the larger of the two.

Removing the KL term changes eight-pass \pk{} by $\gainSmallPkDbVsKlzero$ $\ciSmallPkDbVsKlzero$ points on DecisionBench at 0.8B and $\gainBigPkDbVsKlzero$ $\ciBigPkDbVsKlzero$ at 4B (positive means the term helps; Table~\ref{tab:h2h}), $\gainSmallPkTypedVsKlzero$ and $\gainBigPkTypedVsKlzero$ on typed-decisions, $\gainSmallPkPubVsKlzero$ and $\gainBigPkPubVsKlzero$ on the public menu, and $\gainSmallPkEmoVsKlzero$ and $\gainBigPkEmoVsKlzero$ $\ciBigPkEmoVsKlzero$ on emotion. The term is decisive in one cell, emotion at 4B, where the run without it collapsed, and that is the only KL contrast that survives Holm adjustment; elsewhere it is worth between $-0.6$ and $+2.2$ points and none of those differences survives. The no-KL arm has three seeds on DecisionBench and on JevBench at 0.8B, and one elsewhere. The single-seed emotion result does not establish that collapse is typical without KL regularisation.

The switch compares best answers, which takes two verdicts on the same question, one of them on an answer the system did not serve; a deployment would have to run it as a held-out comparison on a slice of traffic. At 0.8B it turns the add-on off on JevBench, where \pk{} loses $\absSmallPkJev$ points (gain with the switch $\gainSmallPkSwJev$), and keeps it on every other benchmark. At 4B it keeps the add-on on DecisionBench and emotion; it also keeps it on the public menu, where \pk{} is $\gainBigPkPub$ (not significant), and it drops it in most JevBench folds and on typed-decisions, giving up gains of $\gainBigPkJev$ and $\gainBigPkTyped$ that were themselves not significant. The sampled-answer switch diagnostic, computed on the same runs, kept the add-on in $\swOnSampledBigPkDb$\% of the DecisionBench runs at 4B, where the best-answer switch keeps it in $\swOnBigPkDb$\%; the two statistics disagree in the direction Section~\ref{sec:switch} describes.

Those numbers say that fine-tuning on a benchmark's own rows helps on that benchmark; they say nothing about verdicts against labels, and we do not use them to.

\subsection{typed-decisions without Vega's shipped adapter}
\label{sec:noship}
\input{tables_noship.tex}
Vega's released \texttt{typed\_decisions} adapter was fitted on the pool our methods learn from, so on this benchmark ``released Vega'' already carries those labels. With the shipped adapters switched off the released engine scores $\accSmallNoshipBaseTyped$ at 0.8B and $\accBigNoshipBaseTyped$ at 4B (Table~\ref{tab:noship}). From that start eight-pass \pk{} gains $\gainSmallPkNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$ $\ciSmallPkNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$ and $\gainBigPkNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$ $\ciBigPkNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$, the labelled adapter $\gainSmallSupNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$ and $\gainBigSupNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$, and one-pass \pk{} $\gainSmallPkOneNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$ and $\gainBigPkOneNoshipTypedVsNoshipBase$. The gap between verdicts and labels, $\gainSmallPkNoshipTypedVsSupNoship$ at 0.8B and $\gainBigPkNoshipTypedVsSupNoship$ at 4B, is far wider than with the shipped adapter on, which is the clearest demonstration in the paper of what the $\pi(y)$ weighting means: when the released model puts little probability on the gold answer, a verdict on a sampled answer rarely finds it, and labels do.
