\author{Abhijith Neil Abraham \and Fariz Rahman \and Fadil Rahman}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Vega and Laya are small models that answer typed questions (choose one of these options, yes or no, a rating from one to five) and return a probability for every option in a few milliseconds. Each ships with a recipe for getting better at a user's own task, and in both cases the recipe wants labelled rows. A deployed model almost never has those. What it has is the trail its own answers leave behind: the suggested action was accepted or undone, the alert was confirmed or closed. We wanted to know how much of the labelled-data recipe one can recover from that single bit per decision, and we measured it on the benchmarks the two model authors picked for their own READMEs rather than on benchmarks of our choosing. \pk{} leaves the released model untouched and trains a small add-on next to it (a gated LoRA adapter on Vega's decision engine; a copy of Laya's decision head, with or without the encoder) by REINFORCE on the right / wrong reward of a sampled answer, with a running baseline and a KL term that keeps the add-on near the released probabilities. The policy this produces is badly over-confident, so a single temperature is then fitted, also from right / wrong outcomes, using nothing more than the observation that a calibrated model's sampled answer is right as often as it says it is. Over six benchmarks, two Vega sizes, Laya, three seeds and the ablations, 233 runs in all, \pk{} is ahead of VegaML's own test-time-training head on four of the six benchmarks at both sizes even though that head sees the labels; on DecisionBench it gains 10.4 and 6.7 points where TTT gains 2.2 and 0.8. It stays within a point or two of the same adapter trained on labels. On Laya the result depends on how much of the model we let it touch. Training only the head gives $+13.2$ on typed-decisions against the $+40.2$ of Laya's whole-model fine-tune, while unfreezing the encoder under the same one-bit signal gives $\gainLayaEncTyped$ and puts \pk{} ahead of that fine-tune on the public tasks, DecisionBench and emotion. Without the KL term the accuracy gains shrink or reverse; without the temperature step accuracy is unchanged and calibration error is two to four times worse. All base numbers match their READMEs and one script reruns the grid.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A decision model, in the sense Vega~\citep{vegaml} and Laya~\citep{laya} use the word, is a model that takes a situation and a typed question and returns one probability per option in a single forward pass. Which of these five tools should the agent call? Is this message abusive? How urgent is this ticket, on a scale of one to four? Both models are built for the regime where an agent or a pipeline asks thousands of such questions a day on local hardware, and both put some care into the probabilities, because the probabilities are what decide which cases get routed to a person.

Each model comes with its author's preferred way of getting better at a new task. For Vega this is test-time training, a small per-task classifier fitted to the frozen model's features from labelled rows, with adapters trained the same way as the heavier option. Laya's recipe is to fine-tune the whole model on labelled rows with its RLCD objective, and the released \texttt{laya-typed-decisions} checkpoint is what that recipe produces on Laya's headline benchmark. Labelled rows are the common requirement, and labelled rows are what a deployed model does not get. It gets something weaker and much more plentiful. A proposed tool call was cancelled by the user. A flagged message turned out, on review, to be fine. An escalated ticket did in fact need escalating. Every one of these events says whether the model was right; none of them says what the right answer was. In the vocabulary of contextual bandits~\citep{langford2008epoch,li2010contextual} the model sees the reward of the arm it pulled and learns nothing about the arms it did not.

The question we set out to answer is narrow. Can a deployed decision model improve from that one bit per decision, and, if it can, how does the improvement compare with what the model's own authors get from the answer key, on the benchmarks those authors chose? We did not want to pick benchmarks that suit the method, so the test set is the union of the two READMEs (Vega's 43 public tasks, JevBench~\citep{jevbench} and DecisionBench~\citep{decisionbench}; Laya's typed-decisions~\citep{typeddecisions}) plus two ordinary text classification sets with enough rows to study the effect of feedback volume, emotion~\citep{saravia2018emotion} and AG News~\citep{zhang2015agnews}.

The method, which we call \pk{} because the model only ever gets to peek at whether it was right, is deliberately plain. We never modify the released weights. Next to them we train a small add-on: for Vega a rank-8 gated LoRA adapter on the input layers of its decision engine, attached with VegaML's own adapter mechanism; for Laya a trainable copy of its two-layer decision head, and in one variant a copy of the encoder as well. The add-on is trained by REINFORCE~\citep{williams1992reinforce} on the right / wrong reward of an answer sampled from the current policy, with an exponential running mean of recent rewards as the baseline, and a KL penalty towards the released model's probabilities. The penalty matters more than its size suggests; without it one bit per row drives the policy onto a handful of options within a couple of passes. Policy-gradient training also leaves the probabilities far too sharp, and here we had no labels to run temperature scaling on. We fit the temperature from right / wrong feedback instead. A calibrated model's sampled answer is right with exactly the probability it assigned to that answer, so each (answer, outcome) pair on a held-out slice of the feedback is a Bernoulli observation of the temperature-scaled probability, and the temperature that makes the observed record most likely is the one we use. We refer to this as the honesty fix. Finally, for deployments that cannot afford a regression anywhere, a switch compares the add-on and the released model on the same held-out feedback and keeps the add-on only where it was right more often.

Everything is run on Vega 0.8B, Vega 4B and Laya, against each model's own answer-key method, against an answer-key version of our own add-on (same capacity, full labels) as an upper bound, and through every ablation: no KL term, no honesty fix, switch on and off, one add-on per benchmark against one add-on pooled over all of them. Three seeds everywhere, 233 steps in total, scored with VegaML's own vendored benchmark code so that each base model's number can be checked against its README, which it does match.

What we found is roughly this. With one bit per row, \pk{} is ahead of Vega's labelled-data TTT head on the public tasks, DecisionBench, typed-decisions and AG News, at both model sizes; TTT is ahead on emotion, and clearly so. \pk{} lands within a point or two of our own adapter trained on the labels, so on Vega the bit is nearly as good as the label. It is also the only thing in our grid that improves Vega 4B on JevBench. On Laya, the head alone is too small a lever: $+13.2$ on typed-decisions where the whole-model fine-tune gets $+40.2$. Give \pk{} the encoder too and it reaches $\gainLayaEncTyped$ there, and on four of the other five benchmarks it does better than Laya's own fine-tune, which specialises hard to typed-decisions and pays for it on JevBench. The two parts we were least sure about, the KL term and the feedback-fitted temperature, both turn out to be load-bearing.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Learning from bandit feedback.}
Training a structured predictor from the reward of its own outputs goes back at least to bandit structured prediction~\citep{sokolov2016bandit}, and Kreutzer et al.~\citep{kreutzer2018reliability} showed it works with real human ratings rather than simulated ones. The off-policy branch of this literature learns from logged feedback with importance weights~\citep{swaminathan2015crm,dudik2011doubly}. \pk{} is the on-policy case, plain REINFORCE with a baseline~\citep{williams1992reinforce}, and what distinguishes it is less the estimator than the starting point: a model that already has a good zero-shot policy. Our no-engine control (Section~\ref{sec:curve}) suggests that this starting point is what lets one bit per row go as far as it does.

\paragraph{KL-regularised fine-tuning.}
A KL penalty to a reference policy is a fixture of RLHF~\citep{christiano2017deep,ouyang2022instructgpt} and of offline RL for dialogue~\citep{jaques2019way}. DPO~\citep{rafailov2023dpo} and KTO~\citep{ethayarajh2024kto} fold the same constraint into a supervised objective, KTO from binary desirable / undesirable labels. We use the penalty for the same reason in a far smaller model, and we measure what it buys (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Calibration.}
Temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration,platt1999probabilistic} fits a single scalar on a labelled held-out set. Our version writes the same likelihood over right / wrong outcomes of sampled answers. For a calibrated model $\Pr[\text{right}\mid a] = p(a)$ for whichever answer $a$ was given, so each (answer, outcome) pair is a Bernoulli draw with parameter $p_T(a)$ and the labels are not needed. We report calibration as expected calibration error~\citep{naeini2015ece}, computed by VegaML's benchmark code.

\paragraph{Adapting small decision models.}
Test-time training~\citep{sun2020ttt} adapts a model to the test distribution; VegaML's \texttt{fit()} fits a per-task classifier (PCA followed by logistic regression) on labelled rows and keeps it only when it beats chance in cross-validation. Our Vega add-on is a LoRA adapter~\citep{hu2022lora} in VegaML's own gated form. Laya's RLCD trains encoder and head together on labels with a perturbation policy gradient on proper scoring rules.

\section{The two decision models}
\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{Vega.}
Vega~\citep{vegaml} reads the situation, the question and the options with a frozen Qwen3.5 backbone~\citep{qwen3} of 0.8B or 4B parameters, pools hidden states from two intermediate layers, and passes them to what its authors call a physics engine (14.3M parameters at 0.8B, 30.9M at 4B). Each option becomes an energy well in a 64-dimensional landscape; a particle is dropped and allowed to settle; the output is $P(a) = \mathrm{softmax}(-E_a/\tau(s))$ with a per-question temperature read off the particle's trajectory. The backbone was never trained, only the engine and its gated adapters. Vega's own routes to a better model on a task are the TTT head and adapters, both from labelled rows.

\paragraph{Laya.}
Laya~\citep{laya} is a ModernBERT-large encoder~\citep{warner2024modernbert} of 421M parameters, trained as part of Laya, whose input holds the situation, the question and every option, each option introduced by a special marker token. A two-layer transformer decision head scores the marker positions, the scores are divided by a temperature chosen per (question type, option-count bucket), and a softmax gives the probabilities. Training is by RLCD. Laya's route to a better model is a whole-model fine-tune on labelled rows; \texttt{laya-typed-decisions} is that fine-tune on the training split of typed-decisions.

The two models expose the same interface, a typed question in and a probability per option out, which is what let us use one method and one harness for both. They differ in almost everything else, including how much of each model our add-on can reach, and that difference ends up mattering (Section~\ref{sec:laya}).

\section{\pk{}}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}
A decision model is a policy $\pi_0(a \mid s)$ over the options $a$ of a typed question $s$. In deployment it answers a stream of questions; for each one it, or the system around it, commits to an answer $a$, and some time later a reward $r \in \{0, 1\}$ arrives. The correct option is never revealed. From a pool of (question, answer, reward) records we want a policy $\pi$ that is more often right and whose probabilities can still be trusted. In the experiments the reward is computed from the benchmark's gold answer and the answer is sampled from the current policy, so the feedback is on-policy and never wrong. That is an idealisation and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{The add-on}
The released model is never changed. On Vega we attach a single gated LoRA adapter~\citep{hu2022lora} of rank 8 to the input layers of the engine with VegaML's \texttt{attach\_adapter}; it has 409k parameters at 0.8B and 857k at 4B, about 2.9\% and 2.8\% of the engine. Its gate starts open and the adapters that ship with Vega keep working alongside it. The policy is Vega's usual calibrated output computed from the adapted energies with the shipped per-question temperature. On Laya we make a trainable copy of the decision head (type embedding, the two transformer layers, the scorer) and run the frozen encoder once per row, caching its output. In the encoder-unfrozen variant we copy the encoder as well and train it at Laya's own encoder learning rate of $2.5\times10^{-5}$, in micro-batches of 8 for memory, so that \pk{} is allowed to move as much of Laya as Laya's own fine-tune moves.

\subsection{The update}
For a batch of questions the policy samples one answer per question, $a \sim \pi(\cdot\mid s)$, receives $r = \mathbf{1}[a \text{ is right}]$, and minimises
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; -\,(r - b)\,\log \pi(a \mid s) \;+\; \beta\,\KL\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_0(\cdot\mid s)\big), \qquad \beta = 0.1,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is an exponential running mean of recent rewards, $b \leftarrow 0.9\,b + 0.1\,\bar r$, started at $0.5$. The first term is REINFORCE with a baseline: an answer that did better than the recent average becomes more likely in similar situations, one that did worse becomes less likely. The second term pulls the policy back towards the released model. In our pilot runs without it the negative log-likelihood climbed to 7.5 as the policy collapsed onto a few options; Section~\ref{sec:ablation} puts a number on the cost for each benchmark. Training is 8 passes over the feedback rows in batches of 32 with Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$ for the Vega adapter and $10^{-4}$ for the Laya head (Laya's own head rate), gradient norm clipped at 1.

\subsection{The honesty fix}
Policy-gradient training sharpens the probabilities. Before any correction the trained add-ons have an ECE somewhere between 0.17 and 0.35 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), which would make the probabilities useless for routing. Temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration} is the standard remedy and it wants labels. We do not have labels, but we do have the following. If a model is calibrated, the answer $a$ it samples is right with probability exactly $p(a)$. So on a held-out calibration split, 20\% of each task's feedback rows, we sample $a \sim \pi$, observe $r$, and choose
\begin{equation}
T^\star = \arg\max_T \sum_i \Big[r_i \log p_T(a_i \mid s_i) + (1 - r_i)\log\big(1 - p_T(a_i \mid s_i)\big)\Big],
\qquad p_T = \mathrm{softmax}(\text{logits}/T),
\end{equation}
by searching a grid of 61 values of $T$ between $0.25$ and $32$. The temperatures that come out lie between about 3 and 9 (Figure~\ref{fig:switch_T}), which is a measure of how far policy gradient had pushed the probabilities. The identity does not care who chose the answer, so the same fit can be run on logged feedback about the released model's own answers.

\subsection{The switch}
\label{sec:switch}
On the same calibration split we sample one answer from the adapted policy and one from the released model for each row, score both, and keep the add-on only if it was right more often. Nothing is needed beyond the feedback already collected. Every \pk{} result is reported with and without this switch, because it has a cost: on a small calibration fold the comparison is noisy and sometimes throws away a real gain.

\subsection{Pooled \pk{}}
The default trains one add-on per benchmark from that benchmark's feedback. The pooled variant trains one add-on per model from the feedback of all five benchmarks that have a training split and evaluates it on all six. JevBench has no training split, so for the pooled add-on it is a pure transfer test.

\subsection{Cost}
The backbone is untouched and inference cost changes only by the add-on. One row at a time on a laptop CPU, Vega's engine takes 6.3\,ms at the median when released and 6.5\,ms with the adapter at 0.8B, 11.4 against 12.3\,ms at 4B. The backbone itself takes roughly 75 to 245\,ms on a T4. Training on cached features takes between one and fifteen minutes per benchmark and seed on an L40S; the Laya encoder-unfrozen runs took 4 to 15 minutes each.

\subsection{Protocol}
Every method learns from feedback rows (or, for the answer-key methods, their labels) and is scored on test rows. On JevBench the 5-fold cross-fitting keeps all paraphrases of a question in one fold. The feedback rows of each task are split 80 / 20 into training and calibration rows with a fixed hash. Stochastic methods run with seeds 0, 1 and 2 and we report mean $\pm$ s.d. Gains are paired: the per-row correctness of a method, averaged over its seeds, minus the base model's on the same rows, with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval over rows~\citep{efron1979bootstrap}, resampled within task for the macro-averaged public menu. Accuracy, ECE (15 equal-width bins, as in the README), NLL, skill, macro-F1, JevBench's tiers and paraphrase consistency, DecisionBench's domain slices and typed-decisions' soft accuracy all come from the harness's \texttt{score\_metrics}.

\subsection{Baselines}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base model.} Vega 0.8B, Vega 4B and Laya as released. Each is first replayed: we run the frozen model on the cached inputs and check its probabilities against the official prediction call (largest absolute difference $5\times10^{-5}$ for Laya, $0$ for Vega) and its scores against the README.
\item \textbf{Their improvement, from the answer key.} For Vega, VegaML's TTT head (\texttt{fit()} with default settings) trained on the labelled feedback rows of each task. For Laya, the released \texttt{laya-typed-decisions} checkpoint, which is Laya's whole-model fine-tune on typed-decisions' labelled training split, scored on all six benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Answer-key add-on.} The same adapter (Vega) or head copy (Laya) trained with cross-entropy on the labels of the same rows, with the same KL term, followed by ordinary label-based temperature scaling on the same calibration rows. Same capacity as \pk{}, full information.
\item \textbf{\pk{} and its ablations.} No KL term ($\beta = 0$); no honesty fix (the trained add-on at $T = 1$); with the switch; pooled; and on Laya the encoder-unfrozen variant.
\item \textbf{No-engine control} (DecisionBench study only). A bilinear scorer on the same frozen features, trained with the same feedback, KL term and calibration, without Vega's engine.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Compute and reproducibility}
The grid, 6 benchmarks $\times$ 3 models $\times$ methods $\times$ 3 seeds for 233 steps, ran on one NVIDIA L40S in about five GPU-hours from cached backbone features. A single script, \texttt{experiments/peekaboo\_remaining/run.py}, runs every step, skips the finished ones, retries failures and writes the tables; results, logs and the tables in this paper are in the repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/abhijithneilabraham/peekaboo}} Section~\ref{sec:curve} ran on a laptop CPU and was reproduced independently on a second machine (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\paragraph{Against Vega's own method.}
On DecisionBench \pk{} gains $+10.4$ points [$+8.0$, $+12.8$] at 0.8B and $+6.7$ [$+4.6$, $+8.8$] at 4B. VegaML's TTT head, trained on the labels of the same rows, gains $+2.2$ and $+0.8$. On the public menu \pk{} gains $+3.4$ and $+0.1$ while TTT loses $2.3$ and $3.9$ points. The per-task table (Appendix~\ref{app:tasks}) shows where the TTT loss comes from: a logistic head fitted to 50 labelled rows is quite capable of overturning Vega's zero-shot answer on tasks where the zero-shot answer was the better one, and \texttt{fit()} only checks that the head beats chance, not that it beats the zero-shot answer. On typed-decisions the two are close at 0.8B ($+5.9$ against $+5.5$) and \pk{} is ahead at 4B ($+3.4$ against $+2.4$). On AG News neither moves the base model by more than a point. Emotion goes the other way, and not narrowly: TTT gains $+7.0$ and $+7.3$ where \pk{} gets $+2.5$ at 0.8B and loses $2.3$ at 4B. A six-way single-label task with 4{,}000 labelled rows is where logistic regression on good features is at its best, and one bit per row is a poor substitute for the label there.

\paragraph{Against the same add-on with the answer key.}
The answer-key adapter has the capacity of \pk{} and all the information. The paired difference, \pk{} minus adapter, is $+0.7$ [$-0.9$, $+2.2$] on the public menu, $-1.5$ [$-3.5$, $+0.5$] on DecisionBench, $-0.9$ [$-2.5$, $+0.6$] on typed-decisions and $+0.1$ on AG News at 0.8B; at 4B it is $+1.1$, $+1.5$, $-1.1$ and $+0.6$ (Table~\ref{tab:h2h}). Emotion is again the exception, at $-3.6$ for both sizes. Elsewhere the intervals straddle zero, and we read this as the bit being worth nearly as much as the label once the model it feeds already knows roughly what it is doing.

\paragraph{JevBench.}
JevBench has 231 rows, no training split, and asks for judgment under paraphrase. Only \pk{} and the base model are defined on it, since TTT and the answer-key methods need labelled training rows and the cross-fitted \pk{} only needs rewards on the other folds. At 4B it is the one improvement we found on this benchmark: $+4.5$ [$+0.3$, $+9.0$]. At 0.8B it loses $3.3$ points [$-7.1$, $+0.4$]. The switch (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) brings that to $-0.6$ and pooling (Section~\ref{sec:pooled}) to $-0.1$, which is the kind of result that is easy to over-read; the JevBench intervals are wide and we would not build much on them.

\subsection{Calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:ece} and Figure~\ref{fig:rel} show the calibration side. Policy-gradient training over-sharpens: before the honesty fix the add-on's ECE is 0.24 on DecisionBench at 0.8B, 0.22 at 4B, and 0.20 and 0.17 on typed-decisions. The feedback-fitted temperature brings those to 0.055, 0.049, 0.077 and 0.044. On DecisionBench that is below the base model (0.105 and 0.099) and below the answer-key adapter with label-based temperature scaling (0.071 and 0.051); on typed-decisions it is about level with base (0.063 and 0.050). The public menu is the case that does not work. Every trained method, \pk{} included, is worse calibrated there than base Vega (0.203 against 0.154 at 0.8B), and we think the reason is simply that one temperature for 43 tasks whose option counts run from 2 to 151 is too coarse an instrument. On Laya the honesty fix is what makes the trained head usable at all. Base Laya is poorly calibrated on DecisionBench (0.174) and emotion (0.306), and \pk{} lands both between 0.07 and 0.09.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablation}
\input{tables_ablation.tex}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{ablation.pdf}
\caption{Ablations on Vega 0.8B (left) and 4B (right): accuracy gain (top) and ECE (bottom; the black ticks are the base model). Dropping the KL term costs accuracy on five of six benchmarks; dropping the honesty fix leaves accuracy unchanged and multiplies ECE; the switch gives up some gain for safety.}
\label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} and Table~\ref{tab:ablation} take the method apart.

\paragraph{The KL term.}
With $\beta = 0$ the gain drops on five of the six benchmarks at both sizes. At 0.8B it goes from $+3.4$ to $+1.2$ on the public menu, $-3.3$ to $-4.0$ on JevBench, $+5.9$ to $+3.3$ on typed-decisions and $+2.5$ to $-3.9$ on emotion; at 4B from $+6.7$ to $+5.0$ on DecisionBench, $+3.4$ to $+1.1$ on typed-decisions and $-2.3$ to $-5.8$ on emotion. AG News, where the base model is already at 0.90 to 0.93, does not care either way. The KL term also keeps calibration closer to base on the public menu. We chose $\beta = 0.1$ in a pilot where $\beta = 1$ was stable but kept only about a third of the gain, and we never went back to retune it; it is possible a different value would do better on emotion.

\paragraph{The honesty fix.}
A temperature does not change the argmax, so accuracy with and without it is identical by construction (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). ECE without it is 1.6 to 4.4 times higher: on the public menu 0.203 against 0.328, on DecisionBench 0.055 against 0.241, on emotion 0.070 against 0.251, all at 0.8B. Anything downstream that reads the probabilities rather than the argmax, a routing threshold or a conformal set, needs this step.

\paragraph{The switch.}
The switch never turns a loss into a gain beyond base, and it sometimes removes a gain. At 4B it takes DecisionBench from $+6.7$ to $+4.5$ and JevBench from $+4.5$ to $+0.9$, because a sampled comparison on a 42-row calibration fold is a noisy thing. Where \pk{} is hurting it does its job: JevBench at 0.8B goes from $-3.3$ to $-0.6$, and the pooled add-on's emotion loss at 4B from $-3.5$ to $-0.8$. Figure~\ref{fig:switch_T} shows how often it fired. A deployment with more than 42 calibration rows would get a steadier switch than we did.

One add-on fed by every benchmark's feedback (Figure~\ref{fig:pooled}) comes out mixed. At 0.8B it is a little better on the public menu ($+4.0$ against $+3.4$) and AG News ($+1.1$ against $+0.7$), a little worse on DecisionBench, typed-decisions and emotion, and it removes the JevBench loss ($-0.1$ against $-3.3$) without having seen a JevBench row. At 4B, which was winning JevBench on its own, pooling is slightly worse almost everywhere ($+2.0$ against $+4.5$ on JevBench, $+5.1$ against $+6.7$ on DecisionBench). The grid grew out of an earlier DecisionBench study, which measured how the gain grows with the amount of feedback on a fixed 1{,}720-row test set (Figure~\ref{fig:curve}). \section{Discussion}

The case for \pk{} is a model that is already deployed, collects right / wrong events as a by-product of being used, and has to stay competent at everything it already does. In that setting, with the same capacity, right / wrong recovers most of what the answer key would give, the KL term keeps the add-on from wandering, the switch catches regressions, and the honesty fix keeps the probabilities honest enough to route on. The gains were largest where the base model already leaned towards the right option and had it weighted wrong (DecisionBench's online-safety, home-automation and web domains in the earlier study) and smallest, or negative, where the task needs reasoning the base model simply lacks (DecisionBench's logic domain; the temporal-numeric family of JevBench at 0.8B). An add-on of a few hundred thousand parameters is not going to teach a frozen model arithmetic.

The case against it is a fixed task with thousands of fully labelled rows and permission to retrain the whole model. Laya's typed-decisions fine-tune at $+40$ is the clearest example, and VegaML's TTT head on emotion is another. The answer key carries $\log_2 k$ bits per row to \pk{}'s one, and when the task is a clean single-label classification with many rows and the add-on is small, that gap shows up in the accuracy. What the Laya encoder-unfrozen runs taught us is that the gap is partly about capacity rather than about the signal. Given the encoder, one bit per row took Laya's emotion accuracy from $0.597$ to $\accLayaEncEmo$, where Laya's own fine-tune and VegaML's TTT top out at $0.604$ and $0.651$. We do not have the matching experiment for Vega, whose backbone is frozen by design, and we would like to.

The TTT losses on the public menu and DecisionBench have a mundane explanation. A TTT head is a per-task classifier over a fixed label set. On DecisionBench many rows carry their own option list (a rating scale worded differently per row, a list of candidate records to pick from), so \texttt{fit()} kept heads for 20 of the 43 tasks, covering 394 of the 1{,}075 rows, and fell back to the engine for the rest. \pk{} changes the engine, and the engine reads the option text, so it reaches every row. On the public menu, 50 labelled rows per task turn out to be enough for a logistic head to be confidently wrong on tasks where Vega's zero-shot answer was right.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulated feedback.} Our rewards come from gold answers, so they are never wrong, never missing and never skewed towards complaints. Real feedback is all of those things. The method only needs a 0/1 signal, and the code has the knobs for studying the rest (flip probability, report probability conditional on outcome, logged off-policy feedback with clipped importance weights), but we have not turned them, and the results here should be read as the clean-signal ceiling.
\item \textbf{Exploration costs something.} During learning the model samples instead of taking the argmax. Figure~\ref{fig:reward} bounds that cost but we did not design a conservative exploration policy, for instance one that only explores inside Vega's conformal set.
\item \textbf{Hyper-parameters.} $\beta = 0.1$ was picked in a pilot on part of DecisionBench and never retuned; rank 8, 8 passes and the $10^{-3}$ learning rate were fixed before we had any results. We also did not tune TTT (VegaML defaults) or the answer-key add-on separately from \pk{}. The same settings are used on all six benchmarks.
\item \textbf{One temperature per benchmark.} On the 43-task public menu this is too coarse, and it is the one place \pk{} is worse calibrated than the base model. A per-task or per-option-count temperature, fitted the same way, is the obvious thing to try.
\item \textbf{Small benchmarks.} JevBench has 231 rows and 42-row calibration folds. Its intervals are wide and the switch is unreliable there.
\item \textbf{Vega on typed-decisions.} Vega's README reports typed-decisions on six unpublished workflows. We could only use Laya's public release, so Vega's own typed-decisions figure has no counterpart in our tables.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The natural expectation is that the answer key wins comfortably and the only question is by how much. On Vega it mostly does not win. A rank-8 adapter trained on nothing but whether a sampled answer was right comes within a point or two of the same adapter trained on labels, and beats VegaML's own labelled-data method on four of the six benchmarks the authors chose, at both model sizes. The two things we had added as precautions, the KL term and the feedback-fitted temperature, turned out to be where the method lives: without the first the gains shrink or reverse, and without the second the probabilities are off by a factor the temperatures in Figure~\ref{fig:switch_T} make plain. Laya complicated the picture in a useful way. There the bit was not the bottleneck; the head was, and once the encoder was in play the same bit carried Laya past its own fine-tune on four of five benchmarks away from home while recovering most of the fine-tune's gain at home. The open question this leaves, which we could not test because Vega's backbone is frozen, is whether the same holds for Vega if more of the model were reachable, and whether feedback that is noisy, sparse and selective, as real feedback is, leaves any of this standing.
